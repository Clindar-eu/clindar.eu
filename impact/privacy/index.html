What leaves your browser, and how to check

This page is written for whoever has to sign off on running a tool against study metadata. It states what the SDTMIG v4.0 Impact Scanner reads, what it transmits, and how to confirm both from your own devtools without trusting a word of it.

Applies to clindar.eu/scanner. Last reviewed 31 August 2026.

The short version

The Define-XML you drop in is parsed by JavaScript running in your browser tab. It is not uploaded, not proxied, not queued, and not logged. Two controls keep that true and they are different in kind: the page's Content-Security-Policy stops the browser opening a connection to any third-party origin, and the scanner's own code makes no request containing your file, its contents, its name, a study identifier or a result. The first your browser enforces whatever we do; the second is a fact about source code, and the section below is how to watch it rather than believe it.

One thing can leave, and only if you type it: an email address, after your score is on screen, if you join our rules-catalogue mailing list. It goes to /.netlify/functions/subscribe on this domain. Nothing about your scan goes with it.

Your report does not travel either — in either direction. It is written by the same JavaScript that read your file and downloads out of the tab as HTML, Markdown or JSON. We never receive it, which is also why we cannot email it to you and do not offer to.

What a Define-XML contains, and what the scanner reads

A Define-XML is study metadata, not study data: the datasets in a submission, the variables in each, their types, lengths and roles, the codelists and their terms, value-level metadata, comments, computation methods and origins. It normally carries the study identifier and protocol name, and it can carry free text that someone wrote by hand — comments and method descriptions are the usual places.

It does not contain subject data. No USUBJIDs, no visits, no results, no adverse events. Whether your organisation nonetheless treats a Define as confidential is your call, and a reasonable one: it describes the shape of a trial.

The scanner reads the dataset and variable definitions, codelists, value-level metadata and the standards declarations. It reads them to decide which SDTMIG v4.0 changes apply to your study. Everything it reads stays in the tab's memory and is gone when you close it — nothing is written to localStorage, sessionStorage, IndexedDB, or a cookie. Reload the page and the scan is gone with it.

What the scanner page is permitted to contact

Every response under /scanner/ carries this Content-Security-Policy header:

default-src 'self'; base-uri 'self'; object-src 'none'; frame-ancestors 'none'; form-action 'none'; script-src 'self'; style-src 'self'; font-src 'self'; img-src 'self' data:; connect-src 'self'

connect-src 'self' is the directive that matters. It means the page's JavaScript can open a network connection to exactly one origin — this one — and to no other. Not to an analytics service, not to a font host, not to a CDN, not to an error reporter. A browser enforces this; it is not a setting the page can opt out of.

And here is what it does not do. It does not stop the page making a request to this origin. 'self' draws the boundary around third parties, not around us. So the policy on its own is not the reason your Define-XML stays in the tab: it rules out every destination but one, and something else has to rule out that one. A page that stopped explaining here would be overstating its case.

Why that says 'self' and not 'none'. connect-src 'none' is the stricter directive — it permits no network connection at all, and it is what this page would carry if the scanner did nothing but score your file. It says 'self' for one reason: the mailing-list signup that appears under your score needs a request to somewhere, and that somewhere is a path on this domain. We would rather tell you that than quietly ship the weaker policy and keep describing the stronger one.

What the code does with the destination that is left. Nothing. The scanner's own JavaScript contains no fetch call: it reads the file with the File API, parses it in a Worker bundled into its own assets, and renders the result from memory. The only code on the page that makes a request at all is the email widget, and the body of that request is the address you typed — plus a hidden honeypot field, on the rare occasion something automated fills one in. Your Define-XML is not in it. Neither is its name, a study identifier, a score, a band or a rule id; none of them is ever read into anything that could put them there.

That is a statement about what the source code does, not about what a header forbids, and the two deserve different amounts of trust. A header you can read off the wire in ten seconds. Code you have to watch — so the next section is five minutes of watching it, with nothing taken on our word.

The same page also makes no third-party request of any kind — not a script, not a stylesheet, not a font. The scanner's own JavaScript, its stylesheet, the email capture widget and the typefaces you are reading this in all come from this domain. Our marketing pages are different: they use Plausible, a cookieless analytics service, and they load their fonts from Google. The scanner does neither, and neither does this page. That is the whole reason this page serves its own copy of the fonts rather than linking them: a page arguing that nothing reaches a third party should not reach one to render the argument.

Verify it yourself, in about two minutes

  1. Open clindar.eu/scanner and open devtools before you do anything else (F12, or Cmd-Option-I on a Mac).
  2. Read the policy. In the Network tab, reload, click the first document request, and look at the response headers. You should see content-security-policy with connect-src 'self' in it. It is also repeated in a <meta> tag in the page source, which the browser enforces as well.
  3. Watch the scan. Loading the page is itself network activity: you will see the HTML, the scripts, the worker chunk and the stylesheet arrive, which is every visitor collecting the same files. Let that settle. Then tick "Preserve log" and drop in a Define-XML. Nothing further appears. No XHR, no fetch, no beacon, no image request with your data hidden in the query string. The score arrives without a single new request, because the work happened in the tab.
  4. Test the policy — then test the gap in it. In the Console, run fetch('https://example.com'). The browser refuses it and says why: that is connect-src 'self' closing off every third-party destination. Now run fetch('/impact/privacy/'). That one succeeds — same-origin requests are permitted, and any honest reading of the policy has to say so. What keeps your Define-XML out of a request to this origin is therefore not the header. It is that the scanner holds no code that would build one, which is what the step above let you watch.
  5. Watch the one request that can happen. If you join the mailing list, a single POST to /.netlify/functions/subscribe appears. Click it and read the request payload: it is {"email":"…"} and nothing else — no file name, no study identifier, no score, no rule ids. It is the only request the page can make after it has loaded, and it happens only because you asked for it.
  6. Download the report and watch nothing happen. Click "Download HTML report". The file appears with no network request behind it, because the page built it from what was already in memory. That is the same fact from the other side: a document we never receive is a document we cannot email you.

If any of that does not match what you see, we would rather hear about it than not: info@clindar.eu. The configuration is what would be wrong, and we would fix it.

What is collected
The email address you type. It goes to our email service provider and is stored there, which is what a mailing list is; that is the only place a copy lives on purpose.
What our own endpoint writes down
One line per request: the event name, which provider was used, how it turned out — subscribed, failed, rate-limited — and the hour. Not the address. Not your IP address, your browser, the page you came from, or anything you scanned. The address is passed to the provider and dropped; it is never handed to a logging call at all, so there is no redaction step that could one day be forgotten.
Telling repeat attempts apart
If we ever have to distinguish one address retrying forty times from forty addresses arriving once, the endpoint can log a keyed HMAC of the address in place of the address: a short string that cannot be turned back into an email without a secret that never leaves the server and is never printed. It is off unless a specific operational question needs it, and rotating that secret makes every identifier already written unlinkable to any written after.
Why
To send you the SDTMIG v4.0 change checklist, and to tell you when the rules catalogue changes. It is a mailing list: every subscriber receives the same emails, because your scan is not something we hold and could tailor them to.
What is never sent to you
Your scan report. It is produced in your browser and downloaded from it, and no copy reaches us — so no email we send can contain it, whatever you subscribe to.
Legal basis
Consent, GDPR Article 6(1)(a). You gave it by typing the address and asking to join the list. Withdraw it at any time with the unsubscribe link on every email, or by writing to us.
Who else sees it
Our email service provider, as a processor, which stores the address and sends the list. It is never sold, never shared for anyone else's marketing, and never joined to anything you scanned — we do not have that to join it to.
How long
Until you unsubscribe, or after two years without a single opened email, whichever comes first. That is a retention rule configured at the email provider rather than something our code enforces, so it is a commitment we keep by administration — ask and we will tell you what it is currently set to.
Your rights
Access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability and objection, under GDPR Articles 15–21. Write to info@clindar.eu and we will action it. You may also complain to your national supervisory authority.
Rate limiting
The endpoint counts recent submissions per caller for an hour, keyed by a salted hash of the IP address, held in memory and nowhere else. Your IP address itself is never written down, the salt is random per process and discarded when the process restarts, and the counts go with it. Nothing about this is logged.
Infrastructure logs
Our hosting platform keeps request logs of its own, as every web host does. We will not tell you what a platform records by default, because that is its behaviour and not something we can show you in our source code. We treat it as something to configure rather than something to claim: retention is kept short, access is limited to people who need it, and a deletion request is actioned against the provider's stored data and any operational log still in hand. If a DPIA needs the current retention period in writing, ask and you will get the figure we have set.

There are no cookies on the scanner. Not analytics cookies, not preference cookies, not "essential" cookies. There is no consent banner because there is nothing to consent to.
